# LMI Sales Context (v16 draft) — Reference Frame for PREPDO Prompts

**Purpose:** This is embedded directly into every PREPDO AI call (presales prep and meeting analysis), so every report — regardless of which team member generated it — reasons from the same LMI-specific frame, on top of standard SPIN methodology. Built up progressively from scoping conversations, a real sales roleplay, and five real recorded calls. v11 was a major upgrade — three official LMI training documents replaced several inferred/reconstructed sections and corrected an RRR definition error present since v1. v12 added the canonical opening script. v13 added the Probability-of-Close framework. v14 added the RESULT-type vs. BEHAVIOUR-type distinction and the 9(+1) Magic Questions. v15 resolved the COC and MANTRA terms. **v16 sharpens the opening-script talk-ratio note — the first ~5-10 minutes are deliberately telling-mode, so judging talk ratio before the permission-to-probe transition would unfairly penalize a meeting for correctly executing the technique.**

**Guardrail — a pattern to explicitly avoid, seen in the v4 source material:** the ChatGPT example this version draws from confidently guessed which of two real people sharing a name (COO vs. Company Secretary) the LinkedIn URL belonged to, then speculated about that specific named individual's personal state of mind ("what likely keeps a COO awake at night"). PREPDO should **not** replicate this. Stay at the role level ("if meeting a COO of a company this size, common concerns include...") rather than asserting confidence about which specific named person is being met or their personal psychology — especially given the same LinkedIn access limitation applies to us (see the earlier discussion on this). This is a reliability issue as much as a caution: a confidently wrong guess about which real person you're meeting is worse than an honest "unable to confirm."

**Where this actually applies (v0.8 scoping decision):** Presales Prep uses this fully — situational research (web search on the account) plus this mechanic to form PBM hypotheses and opening questions. Meeting Analysis's core scoring/detailed assessment is **generic SPIN-craft judgment and doesn't need this file** — how well the salesperson moved through Situation→Problem→Implication→Need-payoff is assessable on its own terms. Recommended Actions in Meeting Analysis is also generic (transcript-based next-step extraction, Fathom-style), not LMI-flavored — gaps get filled by manual Add Action, not deeper AI-generated LMI content.

## Methodology: SPIN, with LMI's own vocabulary layered on top

Use SPIN as the underlying question sequence — Situation, Problem, Implication, Need-payoff — but reason and phrase output using LMI's specific terms, mapped as follows:

| SPIN stage | LMI equivalent / what to listen for |
|---|---|
| Situation | Rapport, Credibility, Trust established with the contact; role/context diagnosis (decision maker / influencer / coordinator) |
| Problem | Prime Buying Motive(s) (PBM) — the pain the contact names, in their own words |
| Implication | Urgency Built; RRR (Results-to-Resource(spent) Ratio — LMI's ROI-equivalent measure) starting to take shape as a cost of inaction |
| Need-payoff | RRR Established and Verbalised by the client themselves (not asserted by the salesperson) — ultimately expressed as make more money / lose less money / opportunity not wasted / emotional needs met; Quantified Opportunity identified; Buying Signals present |

**Core principle:** don't identify "the decision maker" — identify the "owner of the pain." The person with budget authority isn't always the person who most wants the problem solved. Ask, throughout: who loses sleep over this issue, who gets measured on improving it, who benefits if managers become more accountable and execution-focused.

**EDM (Economic Decision Maker) — a distinct, related concept.** LMI's own term for the person who holds ultimate budget/economic authority for a deal. This is not always the same person as the "owner of the pain" above — EDM is about who can actually release money, pain-ownership is about who most wants the problem solved. LMI's default approach is to target the EDM directly when possible, but **in larger organizations this frequently isn't achievable in a first meeting** — there are multiple layers of decision-making, and a first-shot close is genuinely rare at that scale. This is structural, not a failure of the meeting. **Practical implication for probability-of-close and success-criteria judgments:** a first meeting with a large, complex, multi-stakeholder organization (a conglomerate, a large PSU, a company with 20+ independent business units) should be judged against a different, more modest success bar than a smaller company where the person across the table likely *is* the EDM. "Secured a clear next step and identified who else needs to be involved" is a strong outcome for a large-org first meeting — it is not the same thing as a stalled or weak meeting, even though no close (or even a strong buying signal) occurred.

**EPP (Effective Personal Productivity)** is one named LMI process/product line, distinct from — or a specific application of — the general 5-goal mechanic above. Framed for a business audience as improving planning discipline, delegation, priority management, personal accountability, self-management, communication, and time management at the individual leader level — the same underlying mechanic (self-set SMART goals, weekly accountability, better-than-normal standard), positioned toward productivity/execution outcomes specifically rather than the full business+personal dual-perspective framing. *(Still to confirm: whether EPP structurally differs from the general 5-goal/9-week mechanic, or is simply that same mechanic marketed under a productivity-specific name for a particular buyer conversation.)*

**Discipline: delay naming LMI.** A real, effective pattern — don't mention "LMI" or "training" for the first 20-30 minutes of a first meeting. Stay entirely in Situation/Problem/Implication territory until enough pain has genuinely surfaced. The transition line should describe the diagnosis, not the vendor: *"From what you've described, this doesn't sound like a capability issue — your people are technically strong. The challenge is creating consistent leadership behaviours as you scale. That's precisely the type of problem we help solve."* No mention of training or workshops at the transition point — only business outcomes.

## The actual LMI mechanic (this is the real "black box" — keep it this narrow, don't pad it)

LMI develops leaders through **one integrated process addressing two perspectives simultaneously**: business results and personal life outcomes. The two aren't run separately — the same participant works on both in parallel.

**The concrete structure:**
- 5 live SMART goals, set for a 3-month horizon
- 3 business goals + 2 personal goals
- The standard is not "goal achieved" — it's **better-than-normal outcomes**, precisely defined: the baseline for "normal" is what *that specific participant* would likely have achieved in the same 3 months without LMI — a counterfactual, individual baseline, not a fixed industry benchmark or a comparison to peers. Since every participant's situation and starting point differ, "better than normal" is always relative to their own likely unaided trajectory.

  Example of a specific goal: *"Increase monthly sales from ₹40 lakh to ₹60 lakh by [date, 3 months out]."* The credibility of the claim comes from the comparison being to that individual's own likely path, not an arbitrary target — it can't be dismissed as "an easy number for someone else."

**The USP, translated to language a prospect cares about (not methodology language) — official LMI framing, "Needs = Motives":**
- Make more money
- Lose less money
- Opportunity not wasted (i.e. seize opportunity — same idea, this is LMI's own exact phrasing)
- Emotional needs (a fourth category, distinct from the three financial/opportunity ones — a real, named motive category, not just a footnote)

That's the whole translation layer — when explaining LMI to a business audience, express the mechanic above in those four outcome terms, not in program/methodology terms. Don't over-elaborate the mechanic beyond what's stated here; it's deliberately simple and that simplicity is part of what makes it explainable to a skeptical business buyer.

Process characteristics (secondary detail, use only if asked): the 3-month/5-goal horizon runs through a structured 9-week group coaching cycle — a documented, repeatable process underneath the goal-setting mechanic, not an improvised discussion series. Participants apply their goals to live work rather than case studies, and progress toward the better-than-normal standard is what's measured — not attendance or satisfaction scores. The 9-week cycle is once-a-week group coaching, half-day per session, with the "results visible by 3-4 months" window referring to when the elicited goals typically show ground-level results, slightly past the 9-week program itself.

**Ownership principle (critical to how goals are set — real, verbatim-derived point):** goals must be self-generated by the participant, not prescribed by the facilitator or the company. "If we prescribe goals they don't get ownership. When they come out with the goal themselves..." — this is a real, load-bearing part of the pitch, not a soft nicety. LMI elicits the goals; it doesn't hand them a template. That said, the company/sponsor's priority areas do inform what participants are guided toward — LMI understands organizational priorities first, then elicits individual goals aligned to them.

**Accountability structure:** each goal gets signed off by the person the participant reports to — a built-in check-and-balance, not just a self-set target with no oversight.

**Individual customization even on a shared skill:** if three participants are all working on, say, delegation, their actual block point differs — one may be newly fearful after a bad delegation experience, another may still be micromanaging despite "delegating" in name. The tools surface each person's specific gap rather than applying one generic fix.

**Shared/common goals for cross-functional silo-breaking (a real, valuable flexibility on the standard structure):** when a client's core problem is departmental silos or lack of shared ownership, LMI can set one common goal across the whole cohort (e.g. "reduce order-to-delivery cycle time") instead of purely individual goals — everyone contributes toward it from their own function. This directly surfaces each function's dependencies on the others and builds cross-functional accountability. Worth knowing this flexibility exists — the 3+2 structure isn't rigidly individual-only when the client's actual problem is collaboration itself.

**The weekly debrief as the real change mechanism (not the content, the accountability):** each session opens with a debrief — what happened since last week, what was and wasn't done, what should have happened. Habit doesn't change because someone is told to change; it changes from an internal trigger. A specific, real example of this mechanism: in a shared-goal cohort, a participant realizing mid-debrief "I let the team down" is what actually drives behavior change — peer visibility and self-realization, not instruction. This is worth reflecting in Meeting Analysis "Addressing Missed Items" language when describing how LMI actually works, versus generic "training reinforcement."

## The canonical LMI Sales Cycle (official — this is the master meeting agenda, supersedes the informal SPIN mapping above as the primary structure)

This is LMI's own official meeting structure, in order. The SPIN mapping above is a useful lens on top of this, but this 11-stage cycle is the real, canonical agenda:

1. **Rapport** — if the prospect isn't friendly, won't answer questions, won't open up: get comfortable with each other, break the ice, before anything else.
2. **Credibility** — established across four things: Company, Process, Product, and **You** (the salesperson) — credibility isn't just about LMI as an organization, the salesperson's own credibility is one of the four pillars.
3. **Trust** — arises from sharing client names, testimonials, and **your own self-belief**. (Notable: self-belief is explicitly named as a trust-building input, not just external proof — ties to the "first, persuade yourself" principle under Stalls & Objections below.)
4. **Probing** — surface challenges, opportunities, gaps, future vision; needs, wants, desires; pain points and gain points. This is the formal precursor to naming a PBM — don't skip straight to PBM without genuine probing first.
5. **PBM (Prime Buying Motives)** — find the greatest, most impacting, most urgent motives; specifically look for the ones that **can be quantified** — an unquantifiable PBM is a weak PBM.
6. **Needs = Motives** — the four-part translation: make more money / lose less money / opportunity not wasted / emotional needs. (See the corrected USP section above.)
7. **RRR (Results-to-Resource(spent) Ratio)** — once a PBM is achieved, what is the *value* of that result — this is literally how LMI defines its own ROI conversation.
8. **Urgency** — how fast does the prospect want that result; if they don't act, what do they lose; if they delay, what do they lose. Two distinct framings (cost of inaction, cost of delay) — use both, they're not redundant.
9. **Stalls & Objections** — incomplete information leads to stalls/objections; **the salesperson's own low self-belief can also cause them** (a notably self-aware, non-obvious point — see the dedicated section below for full official handling material).
10. **Closing** — enough buying signals accumulated = time to close. "Ask to buy" — explicitly, more than once if needed. Persistence, not aggression, is the operative idea.
11. **Referrals** — ask, every time; treat it as essential to the business, not optional politeness. This is explicitly how the sales funnel gets fed, not a courtesy afterthought.

**Practical implication for Meeting Analysis:** this 11-stage structure is a better checklist for "what stage did the conversation reach, and what was skipped" than the abbreviated 4-stage SPIN mapping — e.g. a meeting that jumps from Rapport straight to PBM without real Probing has skipped a real, named stage, not just "moved fast."

## The canonical opening script (R-C-T, real language, permission-gated) — this is how stages 1-3 actually get executed

**Sequence: ask permission to talk, deliver the R-C-T opening in "telling mode" (~5 minutes), then ask permission to switch into questioning mode.** This permission-gating is itself the technique — it's not just politeness, it's what makes the subsequent talk-ratio shift (below) feel natural rather than abrupt.

**Permission to talk:** *"Is it okay if I share something about us?"*

**The R-C-T opening itself, in telling mode (real, close-to-verbatim language):**
- *"We are LMI, the world's largest performance enhancement company."*
- *"Our methodology covers two perspectives: the business perspective — getting better-than-normal results — and we get there by addressing the people perspective — building better leaders."*
- *"Our USP is that we deliver measurable results that generate ROI."*
- **Credibility:** millions of participants; present in 90 countries.
- **Trust:** worked with very large numbers of SMEs, and named large accounts — **Aditya Birla, Siemens, Saint-Gobain** (Siemens is a new named account, not previously captured).
- **Trust + Credibility combined:** "many of these have done 500 people" — reorder volume as proof.
- **Trust + Credibility combined, with pricing stated directly as part of the credibility case (not hidden):** *"Mind you, our program is ₹1,50,000 per person, and reorders of this magnitude are a testimony to the value we add and the results we generate that provide ROI on the investment."* Notably, this is the one place pricing is stated as part of a deliberate, scripted trust-building move (high price + high reorder volume = proof of value), not incidentally mentioned — worth knowing this framing exists even though PREPDO still shouldn't auto-generate a specific price figure in its own reports.

**Permission to switch to questioning mode:** *"Is it okay if we ask you about your company?"*

**The talk-ratio target for the rest of the meeting: roughly 20% salesperson / 80% prospect — and the salesperson's 20% should be mostly questions, not statements.** This ratio is explicitly what "allows the SPIN to develop" — the opening R-C-T is the one deliberate exception where the salesperson talks at length, and it's bounded (~5 minutes, permission-gated on both ends) precisely so it doesn't leak into the rest of the meeting.

**Practical implication for Meeting Analysis — a real pitfall to avoid, not just a nuance:** the talk ratio is only diagnostic **after** the permission-to-probe switch. The first ~5-10 minutes are deliberately in telling mode (bounded, permission-gated R-C-T) — a salesperson talking 80%+ during that window is **executing the technique correctly**, not a problem. **Judging talk ratio across the whole meeting, or flagging the opening minutes as "salesperson talked too much," would be a wrong and unfair assessment** — it would penalize a meeting for correctly doing the one deliberate exception the technique calls for. The actual signal to look for: did the ratio **shift** to prospect-dominant once probing started (after "is it okay if we ask about your company?"), or did the salesperson keep talking well past that transition point? Only the post-transition portion of the meeting should count toward a talk-ratio assessment.

## Probability-of-Close framework (official, LMI-specific — resolves the earlier open scope question: yes, this output needs lmi-context.md)

**This settles the question flagged several versions ago about whether Probability-of-Close scoring needs LMI-specific awareness. It does — this framework is genuinely LMI's own model, not generic SPIN craft, so the v0.8 division needs a small explicit amendment: Detailed/Summary assessment stays generic SPIN-craft judgment, but Probability-of-Close specifically should be driven by this framework.**

### Driver 1: SPIN progression depth — where did the conversation actually get to?

- Has a major pain point been **uncovered**? (baseline — necessary but not sufficient)
- Has the prospect **verbalised** (agreed to, in their own words) that pain point? This matters enormously — a salesperson merely asserting the pain point produces a **low** probability; the prospect independently agreeing it's a major pain point **increases** probability meaningfully. The verbalisation, not the assertion, is the signal.
- Has questioning progressed the pain point into a genuine **Implication**?
- Has that Implication been **quantified** into a Need-Payoff?
- **Probability is highest when Need-Payoff has been reached and quantified.** This is the single strongest driver in the whole model — a meeting that reaches quantified Need-Payoff should score meaningfully higher than one that stalls at Problem or Implication, independent of everything else.

**Stakeholder-dependent calibration (important, don't apply one bar to everyone):** in HR-only conversations, a quantified *business-results* Need-Payoff may genuinely never emerge — that's expected, not a failure, since HR often doesn't own business-level numbers. But **if the person in the room is a Business Head, MD, or Owner, reaching a quantified Need-Payoff is expected** and its absence should weigh more heavily against the probability score than the same absence would in an HR-only conversation. This connects directly to the EDM concept above — who's in the room changes what "good" looks like.

### Driver 2: Buying signals — concrete, observable cues, not vague impressions

Ordinary buying signals (each one present nudges probability up):
- Asked about price
- Mentioned who / which level of people they think would be good for this
- Talking about timelines
- Started haggling on price
- Said something like "we think [X level] is good for this" — this also indicates a specific need has already emerged, not just a signal on its own
- Asked how the process is delivered

**Big buying signals (materially stronger than the above):**
- Asked for a proposal
- Asked for references they can talk to
- Gave an indicated (specific, not vague) timeline

### Driver 3: The four-stage mental model — how probability evolves as concerns get resolved in sequence

Think of the prospect's mind as moving through four sequential concern-stages, each of which needs resolution before the next becomes the active blocker:

1. **Problem-solution match.** Is the prospect convinced LMI's approach can actually solve their problem? Once established, this concern is "discounted" and drops off their radar — they've mentally filed it as resolved ("this can work for us").
2. **Price.** Once tackled, this also drops off the radar as an active blocker — though the prospect may still negotiate on it later, that's different from it still being an open concern.
3. **Risk** (of the solution backfiring / not working). This is where case studies and reference-client conversations do their real work — showing case studies **earlier** lowers this concern before it fully forms; enabling a conversation with a past client lowers it **further**.
4. **The ask.** A clear, direct ask to buy, followed by a positive response, is the actual close.

**Stalls and objections act as a temporary dip, not a reset.** Each one lowers probability "a notch" while active. When handled well, probability returns to roughly where it was *before* the stall/objection appeared — it doesn't necessarily push probability higher than that baseline, it restores it. A meeting with objections that were handled well shouldn't score dramatically lower than an equivalent meeting with none — but a meeting with objections left unresolved should.

**Practical implication for scoring:** Probability-of-Close should be assessed as *which stage of this four-stage sequence the conversation actually reached*, cross-referenced with SPIN progression depth (Driver 1) and how many/which buying signals appeared (Driver 2) — not as a single holistic impression of "how did the meeting feel."

## RESULT-type vs. BEHAVIOUR-type challenges (official — a critical distinction, both are needed)

**This is the mechanism behind how probing actually turns into a quantified RRR — genuinely one of the most operationally important distinctions in the whole methodology.**

**RESULT-type challenges** are things the prospect *wants* — outcomes. Real examples: "I want to see increase in sales," "there's a gap between what we expect and what we get," "want to start exports," "want to grow to 100 crore," "want to increase profits," "want to see things happen on time," "we miss deadlines." These are what probing surfaces when asking about vision, opportunities, and gaps.

**BEHAVIOUR-type challenges** are the *why's* underneath the results — root causes. Real examples: people are under time pressure, people don't have ownership, high-stress environment, not enough planning, not organised enough, poor delegation, low focus, too many things at once. **These are exactly what LMI's program actually addresses** — this is the direct link between a named challenge and "how LMI will do it."

**The formula connecting them (official, precise):**
- **Why** (is this happening) → gets you to **Behaviours**
- **What** (is the effect of these) → gets you to **Results**

Behaviours cause Results — asking "because of this behaviour, what happens, what results get impacted" is what turns a behaviour-type challenge into a result-type one, and vice versa, asking "why" on a result-type challenge traces it back to its behavioural root. **Both directions matter, and a good discovery gets both types on the table** — Result-type challenges are what eventually gets quantified into RRR; Behaviour-type challenges are what actually justify why LMI (versus some other intervention, or versus doing nothing) is the right response.

**The "what else?" power question — official technique for avoiding the exact problem Ashok flagged (latching onto one challenge and missing others):** when a prospect names one Result-type challenge, the deliberate next move is "what else?" — asked repeatedly — specifically to surface the *other* challenges they haven't yet mentioned, rather than diving deep into the first one named. This is the direct official countermeasure to a salesperson's natural tendency to grab the first pain point and run with it.

**The canonical bridging question, repeated for emphasis in the official material (ask it of every named challenge, not just the first one):**

*"If we overcome this challenge, what will it result in?"*

This is literally the question that converts a Behaviour-type or Result-type challenge into a quantified PBM/RRR. It should be asked of **each** challenge surfaced, not just the one the conversation happens to settle on.

**A genuinely excellent worked example of quantifying RRR in real time (official, close to verbatim — worth internalizing this exact pattern):**

> "We have low sales" → *(Challenge + Buying Motive)*
> "We are at ₹60cr and want to go to ₹100cr" → *(Opportunity, PBM — quantified target already)*
> Q: "What can stop us?" → "People need focus" → *(Behaviour-type challenge)*
> Q: "Anything else?" → "Yes, they need to plan better" → *(second Behaviour-type challenge — this is the "what else" technique in action)*
> Q: "If they are as they are, what will we get to?" → "₹70cr" → *(this is the honest, unaided baseline — critical, most salespeople skip asking this)*
> Q: "If they plan better, what can they get to?" → "Oh, ₹80-90cr" → **this delta (₹70cr baseline vs. ₹80-90cr improved) is the RRR — ₹10-20cr**

**The technique to extract from this: don't just ask for the target number. Ask for the honest unaided-trajectory baseline first, then ask what becomes achievable if the named behaviours improve. The gap between those two numbers — not the target number itself — is the actual quantified RRR.** This is a sharper, more defensible quantification method than simply asking "what's your goal," since it isolates the specific value LMI's intervention would add rather than crediting LMI for growth that would have happened anyway.

**PBM quality criteria (official, all four matter):**
- Framed as "more of..." / "less of..." / "not missed..." (ties directly to the Needs=Motives categories above)
- Can be quantified — prioritize the ones that can be
- Connects easily and cleanly to an identifiable Behaviour-type challenge — a PBM that can't be traced to a behaviour cause is weak
- **Not COC-caused** (resolved: COC = external Circumstances Outside our Control). A PBM stemming from something outside anyone's control — a market downturn, a regulatory change, force majeure — is not worth building the sales conversation around, because LMI's program addresses behaviour and execution gaps, not external circumstances. If a prospect's stated challenge is genuinely COC-caused, it's not a good candidate for a PBM even if it's real and significant — the program has no mechanism to move it.

**Sample result categories to probe toward (a starting checklist, not exhaustive — described in the source material as a "mantra," meaning simply a powerful, memorable list worth internalizing, not an acronym):** top line, sales, rework, rejections, on-time delivery, cost reduction.

**Talk-ratio staging, refined (adds precision to the 20/80 target already captured):** 80:20 (salesperson:prospect) is explicitly named as the *worst case*; 70:30 is where things stand as probing genuinely "starts." The target ratio comes later, once probing is fully underway — these aren't contradictory numbers, they describe progressive stages of the same meeting, not competing targets.

## The 9 (+1) Magic Questions — official, complete sequence (use judgment — not all 9 need to be asked every time)

1. What do you want?
2. Why do you want it?
3. Why is that important to you?
4. Why don't you have it now?
5. When would you like to have it? Why then?
6. How will you feel when you have it?
7. What do you need to do to get it?
8. Are you willing to do what it takes to get it?
9. What's the plan of action to get it?

**10th (bonus) question, described as especially powerful:** *"What will it mean to you personally to see/have this happen?"*

This sequence moves a prospect from a stated want, through motivation and urgency, all the way to a personal commitment question — genuinely the most complete single probing/Need-Payoff/commitment sequence captured in this document. The explicit official guidance is to use judgment about which ones fit the conversation naturally, not to mechanically march through all nine.

## Real language patterns worth reusing (from an actual recorded first meeting)

- **Framing the core problem LMI solves, at the org and individual level:** "gaps between intent and attainment" at an organizational scale; "gaps between expectation and execution" at an individual scale — targets get set, people strain toward them without reaching potential; that gap is the missed opportunity LMI closes.
- **Differentiating from a performance management system, not attacking it:** "It's alright having a performance management system, but what about enabling your people to perform to it?" — strong line for the "we already have training/appraisals" objection, positions LMI as the enablement layer rather than a competing system.
- **Differentiating from transactional training:** "Most trainings are transactional — good trainers, good powerpoints, then people go home and don't apply it. LMI is transformational — we bridge the gap between knowing and doing."
- **Social proof via reorders, not just testimonials:** citing that an existing client has reordered the program at scale (e.g. 400+ people at one account) is used as a stronger trust signal than a satisfaction quote — reorder volume, not just praise, demonstrates real value delivered.
- **Result variety — RRR (Results-to-Resource(spent) Ratio) is not always financial:** real outcomes cited in practice include cost/capital measures (working capital freed, consumable cost reduction, power consumption reduction) *and* personal-efficiency time measures (e.g. "gained 75-140 minutes of personal efficiency per day") *and* quality/service measures (e.g. zero customer complaints as a stated goal). Don't default RRR framing to money alone — time reclaimed and quality/error outcomes are equally valid and often resonate faster with a skeptical operational audience.
- **Delivery format — lead with value, not efficiency:** willing to run smaller, split, or location-separated cohorts (rather than one large combined group) if that serves the client better, even though it costs LMI more delivery time — "if that comes about by spending double that time, we don't mind... our objective is to deliver you value." Useful line when a prospect worries the vendor will push for a bigger/less-tailored engagement to save the vendor's own time.
- **Natural referral-asking (not just an end-of-meeting checkbox):** a real, effective moment was simply asking, when a related company came up naturally in conversation, "would you be open to an introduction, so we could explore that too?" — opportunistic, in-context referral asks are as valid as (and often less awkward than) a scripted end-of-meeting ask. Ties directly to the "Asked for Referrals" debrief field — the intent doesn't have to happen at the end.

**Credibility/proof point (Situation stage — builds Trust and Credibility early, before the mechanic is even explained):** the underlying methodology has been delivered and tested across 2 million people worldwide. This is a legitimate scale/credibility signal to use when a prospect is skeptical or when Trust/Credibility reads Low-to-Medium — it establishes LMI isn't an unproven or India-only offering, without needing to explain the mechanic yet.

## Standard objection handling

**"We already have training."**
Don't challenge it. Ask a Need-payoff question instead: "How do you currently know whether a leadership intervention actually changed business performance, versus just scoring well on participant feedback?" This surfaces LMI's measurable-results positioning without the salesperson claiming superiority directly.

## Sample SPIN question bank (from a real manufacturing/HR-Head roleplay — adapt, don't recite verbatim)

**Situation:** "How is leadership structured across the corporate office and the factory locations?" / "What role does HR currently play — more operational, or also strategic in leadership development?"

**Problem:** "As you scale, what people or leadership challenges are starting to show up?" / "Do supervisors and mid-level managers tend to escalate issues upward, or take ownership?" / "What happens when a technically strong engineer or supervisor is promoted into a leadership role?"

**Implication:** "If these leadership gaps continue as you grow to [larger headcount], what does that mean for HR and senior management bandwidth?" / "If HR remains stuck solving day-to-day people issues, what strategic initiatives get delayed?" — pause after these; let the implication sit rather than filling the silence.

**Need-payoff:** "What would it mean if leadership capability improved while business results also moved — not just training attendance?" / "How valuable would it be to have a common leadership language across corporate and factory locations?"

## Real objection→response examples (manufacturing/HR context)

- **Time:** "The sessions are structured to respect operational realities — half-day, planned well in advance."
- **Skepticism about training ROI:** "That's exactly why LMI ties learning to measurable business goals, not just behavioral intent."
- **"Factory people won't relate to office-style training":** "The framework is consistent, but the goals are contextual — plant leaders work on plant outcomes, corporate leaders on functional outcomes."

## Sample SPIN question bank — second industry (infrastructure/EPC, COO-level, real ChatGPT-assisted prep example)

(turns a soft leadership question into a number the COO starts calculating himself)

**Differentiating questions (sharper than standard SPIN, provoke deeper reflection):** "What behaviours separate your very best project leaders from the average ones?" / "Which leadership capability becomes the bottleneck when your company doubles in size?" / "If you could change one leadership habit across every manager in the company within six months, what would it be?"

## Signature diagnostic question (confirmed — appears verbatim across three independent real calls)

*"What would you like to see happen in the next six months that is possible to happen but not happening?"*

This exact question has now appeared in three separate real recorded first meetings — two organizational (where the answer anchored the whole conversation, e.g. "cost reduction") and one individual/One-to-One sale, asked as "what do you expect to achieve in the next six months" adapted to the personal-goal context. It's short, non-threatening, and does a lot of work: it surfaces a real Problem without sounding like an interrogation, and the answer becomes the anchor for the rest of the conversation. This is confidently a core, deliberate technique — not one option among many — and works in both organizational and individual sale contexts with minor phrasing adaptation.

## Live tool demonstration — a real, sophisticated technique worth naming explicitly

Rather than only describing the methodology abstractly, when a prospect names a specific problem mid-meeting, demonstrate — don't just describe — how an LMI tool would address it, right there in the conversation. Real example: a prospect described teams working in silos, submitting client proposals separately instead of jointly. The response was to walk through an actual LMI tool live: "What is our mission? What are our principal goals? Who contributes what?" — showing the prospect, in miniature, what a session actually feels like, rather than just asserting that LMI addresses silos. This gives the prospect a genuine preview of the methodology's texture, which a purely verbal description can't do. Look for a moment in any meeting where the prospect names a specific, concrete problem — that's the cue to demonstrate a relevant tool live rather than continuing to describe the program in the abstract.

## Handling sophisticated, high-scrutiny buyers (large conglomerates, mature L&D functions)

A materially different conversation than a growth-stage manufacturing company — the buyer already runs a full internal L&D portfolio, has strong existing vendor relationships, and will test claims rather than accept them.

**"We don't customize" — a real, deliberate positioning stance, not evasion.** When pushed hard on why not adapt the program to the client's own competency framework/values/mindsets, the real answer was direct: LMI stays focused on its own process rather than diluting it, and recommends the client bring in a specialist for anything genuinely outside LMI's scope (e.g. a half-day add-on module) rather than LMI trying to be everything. This is counter to the usual enterprise-sales instinct to always say yes to customization — worth preserving as-is, since diluting the core process to chase every ask undermines exactly the "battle-tested, consistent" credibility the methodology relies on.

**Reframe from "fixing weakness" to "expanding an already-high ceiling," for buyers who are already excellent.** A sophisticated buyer's people may already be very good at execution — hitting 100% of targets. The reframe: "They're good at execution, but might well go to 150%. LMI is about potential, not remediation." This avoids implying the client's people are deficient (which a proud, high-performing organization will resist) while still making the case for the intervention.

**Calibrated honesty — don't oversell against every stated need.** When a sophisticated buyer listed many desired outcomes (strategic thinking, ambiguity tolerance, systems thinking, risk foresight), the honest response was explicit: "Everything is not possible and we will not claim everything will happen" — some of what they described the program would address directly, some only indirectly, and at least one named item ("optimization") was flagged as not a direct fit at all. This kind of calibrated, non-defensive honesty builds more trust with a discerning buyer than claiming universal fit would — a sophisticated buyer will test overclaiming and lose trust fast if caught.

**Delivery-consistency answer, for "will quality vary by facilitator?"** No freelancers — all facilitators carry advanced LMI-USA certification, varied professional backgrounds, but the process itself is fully documented, so a group in Mumbai and a group elsewhere would have the same experience even with different facilitators delivering it. The process is the product; the facilitator delivers it, doesn't personalize it.

**Non-territorial stance on existing assessment tools.** LMI has its own assessment tool but doesn't push it as mandatory — if the client already uses something else (e.g. Hogan), that's treated as good enough. Reduces friction with clients who have existing assessment infrastructure they don't want to replace.

**A clean, quotable founder-attributed philosophy line** (useful as a closing or reflective moment in a report, not just internal reference): *"Our job while delivering the program is to build urges that culminate into desire. Nobody changes because they see a good idea. Nobody changes because you tell them to change. They change when it boils up."* — the sharpest, most quotable version yet of the internal-motivation principle already captured under the debrief mechanism above.

**A real account-continuity risk worth naming.** A prior LMI engagement with this same account (decades earlier) ended not because of program failure but because the internal champion (the HR head at the time) changed roles and the relationship lapsed. Worth remembering when scoping expectations for the account: a program's success doesn't guarantee continuity if the engagement depends on one internal champion — multiple stakeholder relationships reduce this risk.

## A real timing objection — handled by deferring, not overcoming (a healthy example for the Overcome=Partial case)

Not every objection should be pushed past in the same meeting. Real example: a prospect was mid-relocation between offices and didn't want to start a program during the transition. Rather than trying to close around it, the response was to accept the timing constraint and shift to establishing *when* the engagement would start (a specific future month), rather than *whether* it would. This is a legitimate outcome — logged as an objection with Overcome status "Partial" (acknowledged and deferred with a concrete future commitment, not resolved to immediate agreement, but also not lost). Not every real objection needs to be reframed away in the room; sometimes the correct move is agreeing to the constraint and securing a specific future re-engagement point instead.

**A second, sharper real example of the same pattern — asking directly for the checkpoint that triggers re-engagement.** A prospect was already fully convinced ("I have loved the program, personally I am 100% in for it") but had a genuine, named objection: her people were fully committed to an internal cross-functional initiative and she didn't want to disturb that. Rather than accepting a vague "let's revisit later," the response was a direct question: *"When do you think would be a good time? When does your initiative end?"* — which produced a specific, concrete answer: a named review date (a half-yearly review in a specific week) that would trigger re-engagement. **This is the technique to extract from both examples: when an objection is genuinely about timing, don't just accept a deferral — ask directly for the triggering event or checkpoint that will prompt reconsideration.** A vague "check back later" is a weak outcome; a named date tied to a real internal milestone is a strong one, even though neither is an immediate close.

## Pilot cohort strategy — start at the top

Recommend starting a pilot with the **top layer** (HODs/senior leaders), not a broad rollout. Rationale: if the top layer changes, lower levels experience the shift and pull the program downward on their own — often mid-program, before the pilot even finishes. This is a stronger opening ask than "let's train everyone" and directly addresses the common internal objection "this needs to look business-led, not like an HR program."

## Process cadence detail (adds texture to the 9-week/3-month mechanic above)

- Weekly half-day group sessions across the 9 weeks
- Weekly review of progress against each participant's goals
- A mid-point review (~1 hour) where participants share gains/results, with HR and business leadership present — this is where skeptics typically start to convert
- Line managers (e.g. Plant Heads) do a short weekly check-in (~15 minutes) on their people's progress — reinforces seriousness without micromanaging

**Session length is set by group size, not by program type (resolves the earlier open EPP-timing question — not a structural EPP difference at all):**

| Group size | Session length |
|---|---|
| Large group: 10-15 people | Half day (4 hours) |
| Mid-sized group: 5-7 people | Half day (4 hours) |
| Smaller group / One-to-One | 3 hours |

The "3-4 hours" language in the individual-sale call and the "half-day" language in the organizational-cohort calls were describing the same underlying pattern from two different group-size contexts, not two different program structures. **The interaction itself is the mechanism** — LMI workshops are interactive by design, and the value comes from that interaction, which is also why very large groups are avoided (interaction quality drops as group size increases, consistent with the "we don't take large groups" point already captured under Pilot cohort strategy).

## Stalls & Objections — official LMI training material (this is LMI's own trained approach, not just generic craft coincidentally relevant)

**Official definitions:**
- **Stall** = a tentative reason not to buy. The prospect lacks self-confidence, or confidence in you (the salesperson), or both. Not entirely convinced yet, but hasn't said why.
- **Objection** = the prospect is not sold on the program or process specifically — doubts LMI's (or the salesperson's) capability to deliver. A more concrete, nameable resistance than a stall.

**What stalls actually mean (official decoding — use this to recognize what's really being said):**

| What the prospect says | What it may actually mean |
|---|---|
| "I need time to think it over" | You haven't given enough information, OR you've given too much and they need to review it, OR they're simply not convinced |
| "I need to talk to [someone]" | Genuine need for buy-in elsewhere, or a polite way to exit without a direct no |
| "I need to wait for a better time" | "What's the hurry?" — urgency hasn't been established; benefits haven't been made visible |
| "We will let you know if we are interested" | Skepticism — benefits weren't made vivid or believable enough to prompt action |

**A practical technique that complements the official decoding above:** don't accept a stall at face value — ask a question that forces specificity, e.g. "What exactly do you need to think about?" or "What would need to be true for this to be a clear yes?" This is one concrete way to execute the official "listen carefully, unearth the real reason" step below — it directly converts a vague stall into a nameable objection that can then be handled with the techniques that follow.

**Official objection taxonomy — two dimensions, useful for categorizing what's actually being logged:**
- *What it's about:* Objection to Company / Objection to salesperson / Objection to program / Objection to process
- *What type:* Objection to time / Objection to effort / Objection to money / Objection due to past experience

**The core reframe (official):** stalls and objections both indicate the prospect lacks the information needed to make a buying decision. Treat each one as **a cue point to unearth the real reason**, and **an opportunity to help the prospect see it from a different perspective** — not as a rejection. The official guidance is explicit: categorize the ones you hear most often and develop standard responses in advance, rather than improvising every time.

**"First, persuade yourself."** A distinctive, self-aware official principle: the salesperson's own low self-belief can itself cause stalls and objections to arise, or cause them to be handled poorly. This ties directly to the Trust stage of the Sales Cycle above, where the salesperson's own self-belief is explicitly named as a trust-building input.

**The official first three steps for handling any stall or objection:**
1. **Listen carefully** — this gives real clues about what's actually behind it
2. **Acknowledge the prospect's point of view** — find genuine points of agreement before responding
3. **Use softening statements** — restate what they said in a sympathetic manner
4. **Attempt to close, using a trial question** — don't just resolve the objection and stop; test whether it's actually cleared
5. **If it persists, handle again** using one of the named techniques below

**Three official named techniques:**

1. **Convert the stall/objection into a question, and immediately answer it** — just say enough, don't overstate the answer, then follow with a trial close.
2. **FEEL / FELT / FOUND** (official formula, used often, genuinely powerful — worth using close to verbatim): *"I can understand how you feel... I have others who have felt the same way... but they found that by [doing X], it was one of the best decisions they made."*
3. **CONTROL / TELL / ASK / EMOTE** — not a sequential technique like the two above. This is a **personality-style-flexing framework** (generic — related to standard behavioral-style models like Driver/Assertive/Emotive/Analytical): the idea is to adapt communication style to match the prospect's personality type, rather than using one fixed style with everyone. A Driver-type prospect responds better to a more Control/direct style; an Emotive-type prospect responds better to a warmer, feeling-oriented style; and so on. Generic sales-personality-adaptation craft, not LMI-proprietary, but officially part of LMI's trained toolkit for handling stalls/objections specifically — the idea being that a stall/objection response should also be flexed to the prospect's style, not just to the content of what they said.

**Quotable maxims (useful for Addressing Missed Items or reflective report language):**
- *"He who has learnt to disagree without being disagreeable has mastered something valuable."*
- *"Every salesperson has five basic obstacles: no need, no money, no hurry, no desire, no trust."* — a genuinely useful diagnostic frame: an unresolved objection or stall is very often one of these five, even when the prospect's stated words are vaguer.
- *"The real persuaders are: our fears, our appetite, and our vanity."*

**Handling a price objection specifically (two standard reframes, consistent with the official material above):**
1. **Cost of inaction** — what is the pain point currently costing them by *not* acting, month over month? Silence on price often means the cost of the problem hasn't been made vivid enough yet.
2. **Investment vs. cost** — measurable results (the better-than-normal outcomes standard, and the RRR conversation) typically generate returns many times the program investment, reframing it from an expense to an ROI-positive decision.

## One-to-One format — real example (services sector, individual buyer/participant)

A genuinely different sales motion from the organizational cohort pitches above: selling the program to one individual, for their own development, rather than to a company for a group. Real context: a professional running two ventures (a boutique advisory practice and an early-stage AI product) being sold the program for himself.

**Key adaptation: quantification target shifts to what personally benefits the individual, not the company's P&L.** The standard "what would a 2% productivity gain mean across your revenue" style of Implication question doesn't land if the prospect isn't the economic beneficiary of the company's growth — this prospect was salaried, not an owner, so company revenue wasn't his personal motivator. The real, effective pivot: "How do you get rewarded — salary, commission?" then "If you had one hour extra every day, what would you do with it?" — moving the quantification from organizational P&L to personal time and personal-venture growth (the venture he *does* own the upside of).

**When a prospect has two roles/ventures, find which one is more personally "theirs."** This prospect split time between an equity-free advisory job and an AI venture that was his own. Questions were deliberately weighted toward the venture where he had personal upside ("AI will be yours always") — that's where the RRR conversation will actually motivate him, not the salaried role.

**Personal-time-to-business-impact question (a strong, reusable pattern):** "If you reclaimed an hour a day, how many more [clients/deals/whatever their core unit is] could you reach?" — turns an abstract "time management" benefit into a concrete number the prospect calculates themselves (in this case: 4 clients → 7-8 with one extra hour/day). This is Implication and Need-payoff collapsed into one sharp, personal question.

**A closing technique variant, used under real time pressure:** when the call was being cut short by the prospect's next meeting, the close shifted from patient/consultative to a direct personal endorsement: "I strongly recommend you do the program — it changes lives massively." Worth having as an available register, not just the slower consultative build — when time is genuinely constrained, a direct, personal (not corporate) endorsement is a reasonable adaptation.

**An honest, imperfect ending — useful as a realistic (not idealized) data point.** The meeting ended with a classic stall — "I'll have to see... send me the details, I'll check it" — followed by the prospect cutting the call short for an unrelated urgent matter. The stall was **not** proactively probed with a specificity question (e.g. "what exactly would you want to see before deciding?") before the call ended — it was simply accepted, and materials were promised instead. This is a legitimate, common real outcome, not a scripted-perfect one — useful as a genuine example of a "missed opportunity" a Meeting Analysis report should be able to name: an unconverted stall at the close, where a specificity-forcing question could have been asked but wasn't, given the interruption made it understandably hard to do so in the moment.

## Stakeholder patterns in manufacturing/industrial accounts

Likely involved: CHRO, Head of L&D, Business HR Head, Plant/Manufacturing Head (if intervention targets operations), Business Unit President (for larger initiatives). The economic buyer is often the business leader who owns the performance problem; HR frequently facilitates evaluation and implementation without being the final buyer.

## Meeting-close mental model

Aim to leave every first meeting with answers to:
1. Why would this account buy? (What business problem?)
2. Why now? (What's created urgency?)
3. Who owns the problem?
4. How are they solving it today?
5. What's the next meeting, and who should be in it?

A meeting that produces these five answers is a successful meeting, independent of whether LMI was pitched.
